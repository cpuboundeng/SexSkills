# EROTIC CRAFT — MASTER STYLE GUIDE

## Overview

This is the craft skill — the general theory of writing sex scenes that every other skill in this collection defers to. Where `blowjob`, `bdsm`, `foreplay`, and the rest give you the *content* (technique, sensation, vocabulary for a specific act), this skill gives you the *method*: how to build a scene that works regardless of what the characters are doing.

The goal is to make the writing *specific, honest, and hot* — to replace vague euphemism and tired cliché with concrete sensory detail, real interiority, and tension that pays off. A sex scene is a scene like any other: it needs desire, stakes, escalation, and change. It just happens to use the body as its language.

**Key distinction:** This is about *how to write*, not *what to write about*. Pair it with any act-specific skill. If a scene feels flat, generic, or rushed, the problem is almost always a craft problem — and it's solved here.

## The Foundation: A Sex Scene Is a Scene

The single most common failure in smut is treating sex as a sequence of events (kiss, touch, penetrate, climax) instead of a *story* with its own logic:

1. **Desire** — someone wants something (or someone). What is it, specifically? What's at stake?
2. **Obstacle / tension** — what stands between the want and its satisfaction? Time? Distance? Shame? A rival? Their own inhibition?
3. **Escalation** — the want deepens; the obstacle gives way by degrees.
4. **Change** — by the end, something is different. A barrier fell, a secret surfaced, a power shifted, a relationship turned a corner.

If a scene has no obstacle and no change, it's not a scene — it's a description. Find the *want* and the *block*, and the sex will write itself.

## Point of View & Interiority

### Stay inside one head
Smut is strongest in a close third-person (or first-person) POV that stays anchored in *one* character at a time. The reader should experience the scene *as* someone — not as a camera floating above two bodies.

### What does the character *notice*?
Interiority is not "she felt good." Interiority is what *this* character — with *this* history, *this* body, *this* fear — notices, thinks, and can't stop thinking:

- "She'd never noticed the place where his neck met his shoulder before, and now she couldn't look away."
- "He kept his eyes open, because if he closed them she'd disappear, and he couldn't bear it."

### The unspoken
What a character *doesn't* say is as important as what they do. The word caught in the throat, the confession they almost make, the name they almost say. Sex scenes are where masks slip — use that.

### The "surprising thought"
The best interiority is specific and slightly surprising — a character who thinks something they didn't expect to think. It's what makes them a *person*, not a performer.

## Pacing & Tension

### Slow down at charged moments
The rule of erotic pacing: **the more a moment matters, the more sentences it deserves.** The hover before a first kiss, the parting of lips, the first skin-on-skin — these get page space. The mechanics between them can go faster.

### Withhold — don't rush to the payoff
Anticipation is the engine of arousal. Delay is not filler; delay *is* the pleasure. Tease, then nearly give, then pull back. Make the reader (and the characters) *earn* the release.

### The shape of a scene
A well-paced scene breathes — it's not a flat line of intensity:

1. **Quiet** — the ordinary, the before
2. **Tension** — desire surfaces, an obstacle appears
3. **Escalation** — heat builds in waves, each peak higher
4. **The turn** — a shift in power or vulnerability; the scene deepens
5. **Peak** — the climax, earned
6. **Resolution** — the landing, the change, the meaning

### Vary the rhythm
Alternate long, sensory sentences with short, punched ones. A long, lingering paragraph that slows time; then three short sentences that snap. The prose itself should breathe with the scene.

## Sensory Language: Show, Don't Tell

### The five-senses checklist
Every beat should touch at least two or three senses. Don't just say what happened — say what it *looked, sounded, felt, tasted, smelled* like.

- **Sight:** the flush, the arch, the parting lips, the skin, the eye contact, the sweat
- **Sound:** breath, the catch of a voice, the wet sound, the low moan, the silence between
- **Touch:** texture, temperature, pressure, the contrast of soft and hard, the grip
- **Taste & smell:** skin, arousal, the particular scent of *this* person

### Kill the vague words
"Good," "amazing," "incredible," "pleasure" tell the reader nothing. Replace them with *specific* sensation:

| Vague | Specific |
|-------|----------|
| "It felt good" | "The pressure built low and tight, and she had to bite her lip to keep from crying out" |
| "He was turned on" | "His hand shook as he reached for her, and his voice came out lower than he meant" |
| "She was beautiful" | "The light caught the sweat at her collarbone, and he forgot to breathe" |

### Body language over internal labels
Show arousal through the body — the flushed skin, the racing pulse, the parted lips, the involuntary sound — rather than announcing it. The body is the most honest narrator.

## Voice, Tone & Word Choice

### Match the register to the scene
The words you choose set the tone. A tender scene and a rough scene use different vocabularies. Decide the register early and stay consistent:

| Register | Words |
|----------|-------|
| **Tender/romantic** | soft, slow, warm, gentle, beloved, mine |
| **Raw/primal** | hard, fast, wet, rough, take, fuck |
| **Clinical (rare, deliberate)** | anatomy terms — use only for a specific cold/clinical character |
| **Euphemistic** | (mostly avoid) — "length," "core," "flower." Use sparingly and intentionally, or not at all |

### The anatomical word problem
Every writer must choose words for body parts. The rule of thumb: **pick the register that fits the character and the scene, and be consistent.** A buttoned-up character's narration might use one set of words; a raw scene might use another. The only true mistake is *mixing registers jarringly* or *dodging with clumsy euphemism*.

### Voice is characterization
A character's *voice* — in narration and dialogue — should sound like *them*. A reserved character's dirty talk is halting; a confident one's is smooth. Let word choice do the characterization.

## Consent & Framing

### Consent as heat, not as disclaimer
Good smut treats consent not as a legal box to check but as a *source of heat*. The "is this okay?" the "tell me what you want," the nod, the held eye — these *raise* the temperature because they show attention and care. Consent woven into the scene is more erotic than its absence.

### The frame for power play
For scenes involving power exchange, degradation, CNC, or taboo (see **bdsm**, **cuckold-cuckquean**, **incest**), the *frame* — the agreed context, the safeword, the aftercare — is part of the eroticism. Write it in, even briefly. A single line ("they'd talked about this, and she'd said yes, and meant it") keeps the scene consensual and safe rather than coercive.

### All adults, always
Every depicted character is an adult (18+), and everything is fictional. This is non-negotiable — in the content, and in how the content is framed. (See the repo's Content Policy.)

## Avoiding Clichés

### The tired list
Worn-out moves to retire or reinvent:
- "He sheathed himself in her" (vague, euphemistic)
- "Their tongues battled for dominance" (overused, silly)
- "She moaned his name" (fine — but it must be earned, not automatic)
- "He was like a man possessed" (tell the reader *what he did* instead)
- "Waves of pleasure washed over her" (abstract — replace with concrete sensation)
- "Her breath hitched" (not bad — but use it once, not every page)

### The fix
Clichés survive because they gesture at real things. To fix one, *unpack it*: what did the wave of pleasure actually feel like? Where in the body? What did it make her do? The concrete version is always better than the shorthand.

## Scene Structure: The Universal Arc

Every sex scene, regardless of the act, follows this shape. (Each act-specific skill in this collection provides its own detailed arc; this is the underlying template.)

1. **Anticipation** — desire exists before the scene. A look, a memory, a text. The want is established.

2. **Approach** — the threshold. First contact, the first kiss, the first deliberate touch. The scene "begins."

3. **Escalation** — arousal compounds; barriers fall one by one. Clothes, inhibition, distance. The body is mapped.

4. **The turn** — a shift: power passes, a vulnerability surfaces, a secret slips, the dynamic deepens. The scene means *more* than just the act.

5. **Peak** — the climax, earned by everything before it. (See the **climax** skill for the orgasm beat itself.)

6. **Resolution / aftercare** — the landing. The aftermath, the change, the meaning. (See the **aftercare** skill.)

## Common Pitfalls (the quick checklist)

1. **No desire established** — why do they want each other? If the reader doesn't know, the scene is just choreography.
2. **No obstacle** — nothing at stake, nothing to overcome. Flat.
3. **Rushing** — skipping the anticipation to get to the act. The build is the point.
4. **Telling, not showing** — "she felt good" instead of what she felt, where, and what it made her do.
5. **Head-hopping** — jumping POV mid-scene. Stay in one head (or switch at clear, deliberate breaks).
6. **Register drift** — tender words in a rough scene, clinical words in a raw one. Pick a lane.
7. **Cliché** — the shorthand that gestures instead of describes. Unpack it.
8. **No change** — the scene ends exactly where it began. Something must be different.
9. **Skipping aftercare** — the landing is where the meaning lives. Don't cut it.
10. **Forgetting it's these two people** — if any two characters could be in the scene, it's not *these* characters. Specificity is everything.

## The Ultimate Test

After writing a scene, ask:

1. **What do these two people want, specifically?**
2. **What's in the way?**
3. **What does each one *notice* that no one else would?**
4. **Where does the power sit, and does it move?**
5. **What's different at the end?**

If you can answer all five, the scene works. If you can't, the problem — and the fix — is in the answer to whichever question is blank.
