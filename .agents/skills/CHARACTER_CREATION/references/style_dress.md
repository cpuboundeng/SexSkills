# STYLE & DRESS

## Overview

Clothing is the fastest character sketch there is — it's the first thing a lover notices, and the thing a person chooses *every day* to say something about themselves. A wardrobe is a decision, not a default: the buttoned-up one who never unbuttons, the one in rumpled layers and yesterday's shirt, the one who owns the room in a tailored suit. In smut, clothes do double duty: they *characterize* (who is this person?) and they *eroticize* (what happens when it comes off?).

**Key distinction:** This reference covers clothing as **wardrobe — who the person is**. Clothing as *erotic reveal* (the peek, the flash, the fabric peeled away) belongs to `exhibitionism-voyeurism` (see `references/clothing_peeks.md`) and `foreplay`. Here, the question is: *what do they wear, and what does it mean?* The reveal is where this reference hands off.

## What Clothes Say (the character in the outfit)

Every wardrobe choice is a signal. Read it the way a lover would:

- **Status & class** — the tailor-made vs. the thrifted, the watch, the shoes. Money, or the lack of it, is written on the body before anyone speaks.
- **Profession & context** — the uniform, the suit, the scrubs, the coveralls, the apron. What someone wears to *work* is a whole backstory.
- **Conformity vs. rebellion** — the person who matches the dress code perfectly and the one who breaks it on purpose. The one who *follows rules* and the one who makes the rules irrelevant.
- **Comfort vs. armor** — some people dress for ease; others dress to be *seen* (or to not be seen). Armor is a character trait, not a fashion note.
- **Era & culture** — period clothing, cultural dress, subculture uniforms (the punk, the preppy, the goth, the minimalist). Style places a person in a world.

## Signature Pieces (the item that *is* the character)

Give each character one item they return to — the thing a lover would recognize from across a room:

- The leather jacket, the tailored coat, the worn hoodie with the frayed cuffs.
- The shirt always unbuttoned one button too far, or always to the collar.
- The jewelry they never take off; the watch they're always checking; the ring on a chain.
- The shoes that tell the story — scuffed work boots, heels that click like a warning, bare feet by choice.

**The rule:** one signature piece is worth ten generic items. A character whose defining garment is *specific* ("the denim jacket with the paint stain on the cuff") is instantly real.

## How They Wear It (style is behavior, not inventory)

The same outfit reads differently on different bodies and different people:

- **Precise** — everything in place, tucked, creased, intentional. Control made visible.
- **Rumpled / effortless** — the untucked shirt, the rolled sleeves, the loosened tie. Ease, or its performance.
- **Layers** — the person who keeps a barrier (a cardigan, a jacket, a scarf) between them and the world.
- **Half-undone** — the button they *didn't* do, the strap slipping, the collar askew. Either carelessness or an invitation.
- **Fidget** — the one who tugs a hem, adjusts a cuff, smooths a wrinkle. Nervousness and vanity are both told in the hands.

## Body + Clothing (the "desire has a face" principle, applied to fabric)

Clothes only come alive against the *specific body* wearing them. This is where wardrobe meets the core character-creation principle — write the clothing through a hungry gaze:

- The shirt that *pulls* at the shoulders, the dress that *clings* where it shouldn't, the trousers that *fit too well*.
- The fabric that's *strained*, *worn soft*, *warm from the body*, *damp with heat*.
- The sliver of skin at a gap, the collar that shows the throat, the hem that rides up when they move. (The *peek* — see `exhibitionism-voyeurism/references/clothing_peeks.md`.)

The point isn't to catalog the outfit; it's to show how the clothes *sit* on this person and what they do to the one looking.

## Underwear & Lingerie (characterization *and* reveal)

Underwear is where wardrobe and eroticism meet — it's chosen to be *seen* (or not), so it's the most revealing item a character owns:

- **The utilitarian** — plain, comfortable, practical. Either self-possession or self-effacement.
- **The deliberate** — lace, silk, the matching set, the thing chosen *in case*. Readiness, hope, intent.
- **The armor** — the expensive lingerie that's about *power*, not just beauty.
- **The contrast** — the buttoned-up exterior with something devastating underneath; the secret the clothes keep.
- **The gone / the absent** — going without, the absence itself the statement.

## Vocabulary

- **The wardrobe:** *suit, coat, jacket, shirt, blouse, dress, skirt, trousers, jeans, hoodie, sweater, uniform, dress-down, dressed-up*
- **The fit:** *tailored, fitted, loose, baggy, rumpled, crisp, creased, worn, soft, second-skin, strained, clinging*
- **The detail:** *collar, cuff, hem, neckline, seam, button, zip, lace, silk, leather, denim, wool, linen*
- **The wear:** *tucked, untucked, rolled, loosened, unbuttoned, half-undone, pulled, slipped, peeled, shrugged off, shrugged on*
- **The read:** *put-together, disheveled, elegant, practical, armor, invitation, uniform, costume, disguise*

## Common Pitfalls

1. **The outfit catalog.** Listing the clothes ("she wore a red dress, black heels, a gold necklace") with no meaning attached. Clothes should *say* something.
2. **Clothing as pure decoration.** If the outfit doesn't reveal character, it's set dressing. Every garment is a choice — show what the choice says.
3. **Ignoring the body under it.** Fabric only matters against a body. Write the *fit*, the *strain*, the *warmth*.
4. **Static dressing.** A character described once, fully clothed, and never thought about again. Let the clothes *move* — shrug, slip, loosen, come off.
5. **Same wardrobe for everyone.** If every character is generically "well-dressed," none of them is a person. Vary the signatures.
6. **Forgetting the undressing.** Clothing's whole erotic point is the *removal*. Hand off to `foreplay` (the slow reveal) or `exhibitionism-voyeurism` (the peek) before the wardrobe wears thin.

## Scene Structure Example

1. **The Entrance** — They arrive, and the outfit speaks first: the coat, the shoes, the one signature piece. The POV character clocks it instantly.

2. **The Detail** — A single thing the lover notices: the way the shirt strains at the shoulders, the sliver of skin at the collar, the ring on the chain.

3. **The Meaning** — The outfit is *read*: put-together, armored, half-undone, an invitation. The POV character draws a conclusion — right or wrong.

4. **The Movement** — The clothes *act*: a sleeve pushed up, a button undone, a hem riding high. The body underneath starts to show.

5. **The Reveal (handoff)** — The first piece actually comes off — the jacket, the shirt, the lingerie beneath. From here it's `foreplay` or `exhibitionism-voyeurism` territory.

## Quick Reference: Scene Arc

1. **Entrance** — the outfit speaks first
2. **Detail** — the one thing noticed
3. **Meaning** — the outfit read as character
4. **Movement** — the clothes act, the body shows
5. **Reveal** — the first piece comes off (handoff to foreplay / the peek)
