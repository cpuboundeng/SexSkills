# SETTING & WORLD

## Overview

Every character lives somewhere and some*when* — and that world decides what's possible, what's forbidden, and what's charged. A kiss in a contemporary office and a kiss in a Victorian parlor are two different scenes, not because the kiss changes but because the *world* around it does. Setting is the container that gives desire its stakes.

**Key distinction:** Setting is **context, not backdrop.** It isn't wallpaper; it's the set of *rules* the characters live under — what they can want openly, what they must hide, what it would cost to be caught. Decide the world first, and the desire gets its shape from it.

## Why Setting Matters

- **It sets the rules.** What's normal, what's taboo, who can be together, what a relationship costs. The stricter the world, the hotter the transgression.
- **It gives the stakes.** In a world that forbids the desire, wanting it is dangerous — and danger is heat.
- **It shapes the body and the voice.** A character's speech, clothes, manners, and even their body (what work, climate, and era have done to it) come from their world.
- **It decides what's *possible*.** Magic, monsters, futuristic tech, period constraints — each opens and closes doors.

## Contemporary (the default — but not generic)

Modern-day realism is the default setting of most smut, and its power is *familiarity* — the reader already knows the rules, so you can spend the words on the people:

- **Still be specific.** "Modern day" is not a setting. A character in a small town, a big city, a specific industry, a particular neighborhood, in a particular year — that's a world. Name it.
- **The contemporary taboo** — even now, some desires are forbidden: the affair (see `infidelity`), the power imbalance at work, the age gap, the "wrong" person. The modern world still has rules to break; find them.
- **Subculture as world** — the punk scene, the church, the office, the military base, the small town where everyone knows. A subculture is a micro-world with its own rules, and it's a great way to ground a contemporary character.

## Historical (the charge of the forbidden)

Period settings do one thing extremely well: they raise the cost of desire, which raises the heat:

- **Research the rules.** What was actually forbidden — and what was quietly common. The corset, the chaperone, the arranged marriage, the "confirmed bachelor," the letters that had to be burned. Accuracy is the respect that makes the transgression land.
- **The taboo is the engine.** A stolen glance, a bare ankle, a hand held under a table — in a strict era, the smallest gestures carry enormous weight. The *restriction* is the aphrodisiac.
- **Voice and detail** — the era lives in word choice, manners, and objects (the carriage, the telegram, the gas lamp). Drip these; don't lecture. (See `personality_backstory.md` on voice and `style_dress.md` on period clothing.)
- **Avoid the costume-party trap** — a character in a corset who thinks and speaks like a modern person is not a historical character. The world must shape the *mind*, not just the wardrobe.

## Fantasy & Sci-Fi (the world as license)

Speculative settings let you change the *rules themselves* — and that's their gift to smut:

- **World rules, not world trivia.** Decide the handful of rules that actually matter to the story: how magic works, what the tech can do, who holds power, what's taboo *in this world*. You don't need a codex; you need the rules the desire will press against.
- **New bodies, new desire** — non-human bodies, transformation, telepathy, magic that senses desire, tech that connects people across distance. The setting *is* the kink, sometimes. (See `monster-tentacle` for non-human anatomy.)
- **New power structures** — a fantasy kingdom with an arranged royal marriage, a sci-fi society with alien customs about sex, a world where telepathy makes secrecy impossible. The invented taboo is just as hot as the real one.
- **Ground it in the familiar** — even the wildest world needs one human anchor (a specific room, a specific meal, a specific fear) so the reader can feel it.

## Monster & Otherworldly (the non-human lover)

When the world includes monsters, demons, cryptids, or aliens, the setting is doing double duty:

- **The world licenses the creature.** What is this being, where did it come from, what are its rules and hungers? The world-building *is* the character-building for the non-human partner. (See `monster-tentacle` for the full anatomy and dynamics.)
- **The human's world vs. the creature's world** — the collision (a human drawn into the monster's realm, or the monster crossing into ours) is the engine. Whose rules apply?
- **Transgression without real-world harm** — fantasy lets you explore "forbidden" desires (the monstrous, the impossible, the dangerous) safely, because the creature isn't real. Use that license deliberately.

## The World as Obstacle

Setting is one of the cheapest, most effective obstacles in the desire engine (see the main skill):

- **The world says no** — the marriage, the law, the family, the class line, the era's morality, the taboo. The desire exists *against* the world, and that friction is the story.
- **The secret the world forces** — what must they hide, and what does hiding do to them? A desire that must stay secret is a desire with teeth.
- **The cost of being caught** — what actually happens if the world finds out? Ruin, exile, death, or just a very awkward family dinner. Know the cost; it sets the stakes.

## Time & Place (the concrete details)

Ground the world in the specific, not the generic:

- **Season & weather** — the heat of summer, the snowed-in cabin, the rain against the window. Weather is mood and plot (stranding, undressing, huddling).
- **The specific place** — not "a house" but *this* room, with its particular light, smell, and objects. A world is felt through its rooms.
- **The era's texture** — the music playing, the food, the technology, the news in the background. A few precise details anchor a whole era.

## Vocabulary

- **The worlds:** *contemporary, modern, historical, Victorian, Regency, medieval, fantasy, urban fantasy, sci-fi, space, monster, supernatural, small-town, city, workplace, subculture*
- **The rules:** *taboo, forbidden, scandal, ruin, the law, the family, the church, the crown, the corporation, what's allowed, what must be hidden*
- **The stakes:** *the cost, the risk, being caught, the consequence, the fall, the ruin, the exile, the secret*
- **The anchor:** *this room, the season, the weather, the era's texture, the specific place, the human detail*

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Setting as backdrop** — a story that could happen anywhere, with the world never touching the characters. Let the world constrain and charge the desire.
2. **"Modern day" as a non-setting** — no place, no time, no specificity. Ground it.
3. **Historical costume-party** — period clothes with modern minds. The era must shape the character's thoughts and fears.
4. **World-building overload** — a codex of lore before any human heat. Decide the few rules that matter, and show the rest through the scene.
5. **No obstacle from the world** — a setting with nothing forbidden, nothing at stake. Give the world a rule the desire must break.
6. **The generic fantasy default** — elves and dragons with nothing original. Invent one specific rule that changes desire, and build from there.
