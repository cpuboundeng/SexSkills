# RACE & ETHNICITY

## Overview

A character's race and ethnicity are part of who they are — and in fiction, they should be written with the same specificity, care, and *desire* as everything else. The goal is a character who is a **full, individual person** — never a stereotype, a prop, or an "exotic" fantasy. This guide covers how to describe diverse bodies respectfully and vividly, and how to write heritage as context rather than costume.

**Key distinction:** Race/ethnicity is a *facet* of a character, not their personality and not their kink (unless their own relationship to it is the story they're telling). Write the *person*, and let their background be one thread of a whole cloth.

## Skin Tone (vocabulary that respects)

Describe skin color with **specific, warm, non-food descriptors** whenever possible. Food words (chocolate, caramel, mocha, coffee) are widely used but often criticized for reducing a person to something to be consumed — prefer concrete and neutral language:

- **Deep/dark tones:** deep brown, rich brown, dark brown, ebony, deep umber, very dark, near-black in certain light
- **Warm/medium tones:** brown, warm brown, golden-brown, medium brown, bronze, copper, mahogany, sienna, tawny, sun-darkened
- **Light tones:** light brown, tan, olive, golden, honey-toned, fair, pale, freckled, sun-kissed

**How to use it well:**
- Describe skin the way you'd describe any feature — as *texture, warmth, and light*, not as a category. "Warm brown, darker across the knuckles," "the pale underside of her wrist," "gold in the summer sun."
- Don't describe *only* characters of color by their skin while leaving white characters as the unmarked default. Be consistent and specific about everyone — or no one.
- Mention skin once or twice, through the POV character's noticing, not as a data point.

## Hair (texture & style)

- **Texture:** straight, wavy, curly, coily, kinky. Describe curls and coils with warmth and specificity: *tight coils, loose waves, springy curls, corkscrew curls, soft spirals* — never "wild," "unruly," or "nappy" as negative.
- **Style:** natural afro, braids, box braids, cornrows, locs, twists, a silk press, waves, a fade, a bun, a buzz. Research the style; name it correctly; respect that some styles are cultural, not costume.
- **Color:** black, deep brown, brown, auburn, chestnut, honey, red, blond, gray, silver, white — or dyed any color. Hair moves, catches light, feels a certain way under a hand; write that.

## Features & Face

- Describe facial features as *a person's features* — a strong nose, full lips, high cheekbones, hooded eyes, a wide smile — without making them into "ethnic markers" or caricature.
- The rule: a nose is a nose, lips are lips, eyes are eyes. Describe them as they *are on this person*, with the same warmth you'd give anyone, not as shorthand for a group.

## Culture as Context, Not Costume

Heritage is more than appearance. Ground a character in **specific, researched cultural detail** — but use it to build a person, not to decorate:

- **Real texture:** family, food, language or phrases, faith or its absence, tradition, community, music, holidays, the neighborhood they grew up in, the way they talk to their mother.
- **Not costume:** a character reduced to a few "exotic" signifiers (a random word in another language, a dish name, an accent) with nothing else inside. If you can't fill in *who they are* beyond the signifiers, you don't have a character yet.
- **Do the work:** if you're writing a background you don't share, research it — names, customs, nuances — and be willing to revise. Accuracy is respect.

## Avoiding Stereotypes (name them to avoid them)

Some tropes are so common they're almost automatic. Write against them deliberately:

- **The "exotic" gaze** — treating a character of color as mysterious, forbidden, or a novelty *because* of their race.
- **Hypersexualized Black characters** — or the reverse: the desexualized "mammy"/"eunuch" roles.
- **The submissive/geisha Asian** — or the "dragon lady"/"model minority" extremes.
- **The "spicy" Latina** — fiery, tempestuous, reduced to passion.
- **The mystical or vanishing Native** — spiritualized, or frozen in a romanticized past.
- **The "one of the good ones" / whitewashing** — writing a character of color with no cultural specificity at all, essentially white with a name change.

The common thread: these reduce a person to a *function* of their race. The fix is always the same — make them an individual with wants, flaws, humor, and history.

## The Desire Frame

A character of color deserves to be desired **as themselves, on their own terms** — not *because of* their race.

- **Attraction to an individual** is fine. "I want *her*" is the target.
- **Fetishizing a group** is the trap. "I've always wanted to try a ___," "I have a thing for ___ girls" — reducing a person to their race and treating them as a bucket-list item — is dehumanizing, even in fantasy. Write the *individual's* draw, not the category's.
- **Characters can have preferences** (types, features, dynamics) — that's realistic. The line is crossed when the preference *replaces* the person with the category.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Food-based skin descriptors by default** — occasionally fine, but as a reflex it objectifies. Reach for umber, bronze, mahogany, warm brown.
2. **Only describing skin/hair for non-white characters** — the "default white" problem. Be specific about everyone, or no one.
3. **Stereotype as shortcut** — using a trope instead of building a person.
4. **Culture as costume** — a few signifiers with nothing inside.
5. **Fetishizing instead of desiring** — "I want to try one" vs. "I want *her*."
6. **No research** — wrong names, wrong customs, caricature. Accuracy is respect.
