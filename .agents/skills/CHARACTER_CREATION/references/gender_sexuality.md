# GENDER & SEXUALITY

## Overview

Gender and sexuality are part of who a character *is* — but they are not a character's whole personality, and they are not the story's whole plot. The goal here is to write characters whose identity is handled with accuracy and respect, while staying focused on what this library is for: desire, chemistry, and a good scene.

Write gender and sexuality as **facts about the person**, not as their defining trait, their punchline, or their "twist." A gay man is a man who is attracted to men — and also whatever else he is (anxious, funny, ambitious, guarded). A trans woman is a woman — and her transness is one part of her, not the reason she exists on the page.

**Key distinction:** Identity is *context*, not *content*. It shapes how a character experiences desire, but it should never reduce them to a category.

## Gender Identity

- **Cis** — gender aligns with the sex assigned at birth.
- **Trans** — gender differs from the sex assigned at birth. A trans woman is a woman; a trans man is a man. This is not up for debate in the text.
- **Nonbinary** — outside or between the binary; may use they/them or other pronouns.
- **Genderfluid / agender** — a gender that shifts, or the absence of gender.

### Pronouns
- Use the character's pronouns correctly and consistently, in narration and dialogue. A single slip in the *text* (as opposed to a character's in-character error) breaks trust with the reader.
- They/them is grammatically fine and has been for centuries; don't treat it as a novelty.

### Writing trans & nonbinary characters
- **Their gender is not a plot twist.** Don't structure the scene around a "reveal" of their genitals or history. Write them as the person they are.
- **Don't reduce them to anatomy.** A trans character's body is whatever their body is — and in smut, you'll describe it like any other body: through desire, with care, using their words for themselves. When in doubt, write around the specifics and focus on sensation and feeling (see the act skills).
- **No slurs, no fetishizing.** Terms like "trap," "shemale," "tranny" are slurs — never in the narrator's voice (and only in a character's voice if you're *deliberately* writing that character as a bigot). Don't frame a trans person as a curiosity or an experiment.
- **Let them be ordinary too.** A trans character can be confident or shy, kinky or vanilla, the dom or the sub. Their transness is one thread, not the whole cloth.

## Sexuality & Orientation

- **Gay / lesbian** — attraction to the same gender.
- **Bi / pan** — attraction to more than one gender; pan often emphasizes attraction regardless of gender.
- **Straight** — attraction to a different gender.
- **Queer** — an umbrella and a self-chosen identity; use it if the character would.
- **Ace-spectrum** — asexual, demisexual, gray-asexual: little or conditional sexual attraction. Asexual ≠ aromantic, and an ace character can still want sex, intimacy, or neither. Writing an ace character in smut is an opportunity, not a contradiction.
- **Aromantic** — little or no romantic attraction; can still be sexual.

### Orientation is not a personality
- A gay character is not automatically flamboyant; a lesbian is not automatically "one of the guys"; a bi character is not automatically promiscuous or "confused." These are stereotypes, not characterization.
- Romantic and sexual orientation can differ (a biromantic asexual, a homoromantic bisexual). Being specific about this adds depth.

## How Identity Shapes Desire (story fuel)

The *useful* part of gender and sexuality for a smut writer is the tension it can create — always handled with respect, never as tragedy-porn or caricature:

- **The newly-out character** — first experiences, the thrill and terror of finally wanting openly. Rich with vulnerability.
- **The character whose desire conflicts with their self-image** — the "straight" character who can't stop thinking about someone. The gap between who they *think* they are and what they *want* is pure engine (see the main skill's desire engine).
- **The character who's still learning their own body** — discovery, awkwardness, tenderness.
- **The character whose identity gives them a specific confidence** — someone who fought to become themselves and now owns their desire without apology.

The point: identity becomes erotic *material* when it's written as a lived, specific thing — not when it's announced like a label.

## Vocabulary & Framing

- **Respectful, current terms:** *trans woman, trans man, nonbinary, genderfluid, they/them, gay, lesbian, bisexual, pansexual, queer, asexual, demisexual, aromantic.*
- **Avoid in the narrator's voice:** slurs, "biologically male/female" (use AMAB/AFAB or describe the specific body), "a transgender" (it's an adjective, not a noun), "both genders."

## Common Pitfalls

1. **The "reveal" structure.** Identity as a shocking twist. Write them as a person first.
2. **Reducing to anatomy.** Defining a trans character by their genitals. Focus on sensation and personhood.
3. **Stereotype as personality.** "Gay = flamboyant," "bi = greedy," "lesbian = butch only." Give them a real personality.
4. **Slurs or fetishizing language** even "innocently" in narration. It reads as authorial, not character voice.
5. **Making identity the whole plot.** If the only thing about a character is their identity, they're not a character yet — layer the rest.
