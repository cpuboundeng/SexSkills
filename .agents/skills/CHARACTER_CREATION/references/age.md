# AGE

## Overview

Age is a fact about a character, not a number on a profile — and it's one of the most powerful levers a smut writer has, because age *is* experience, confidence, power, and hunger all braided together. A character's age tells you how long they've lived with their own desire: whether it's still new and terrifying, whether they've made peace with it, or whether they've spent decades wanting something they never let themselves have.

**Key distinction:** Age is not a stat; it's a *life stage*. Two 40-year-olds can be completely different people — what matters is not the number but what the years have done: the experience gained, the confidence (or regret), the walls built, the hunger refined or buried. Write the stage, not the birthday.

## The Adult Frame (read this first)

Every character in this library is **an adult — 18 or older — and is written as an adult.** That's not a limit on the fiction; it's the boundary that makes the fiction possible.

- **No minors, ever.** Not as characters, not as backstory partners, not in fantasy, not "just this once." This is a hard line, not a style choice.
- **Age-play is not age.** Caregiver/little, "daddy/mommy" dynamics, regression play — these are *roles* two adults step into, not depictions of actual minors. The characters are adults; the dynamic is a game. Write the adults, and keep the game explicitly a game. (See `kinks.md` and `bdsm`.)
- **Age gaps are fine — between adults.** A 25-year-old and a 45-year-old is a rich, charged dynamic. A gap only becomes a problem if a character is under 18. Keep everyone adult, and the gap is material, not a violation.

## Life Stages (what each one gives the writer)

Age changes what a character wants, knows, and fears. Use the stage, not the number:

| Stage | What it usually gives the scene |
|-------|--------------------------------|
| **18–24 — new adulthood** | firsts, discovery, nerves, big hunger and no map for it; the thrill of a body still being figured out |
| **25–34 — the settling years** | confidence arriving, past lovers as reference points, the first real reckoning with what they actually want vs. what they thought they should |
| **35–49 — mid-life** | self-knowledge, defined taste, the "I'm done pretending" decade; often the most honest, unashamed desire — and the mid-life ache of "is it too late" |
| **50+ — late hunger** | a lifetime of wanting, refined or suppressed; the eroticism of knowing exactly what they want and finally taking it; long marriages, second chances, reinvention |

These aren't cages — a sheltered 40-year-old and a worldly 22-year-old both exist. The stage is a *default texture*, not a rule.

## Age as Experience & Confidence

Age is the single best shorthand for *how much of this a character has done before* — and how they feel about wanting it:

- **Inexperienced** — the firsts, the nerves, the tenderness (or awkwardness) of learning a body — theirs or a partner's. Youthful inexperience is fresh; older inexperience (the late bloomer, the repressed one) is its own, richer ache.
- **Experienced** — ease, skill, a body that knows what it wants and asks for it without apology.
- **Confidence vs. performance** — confidence can be real (earned over years) or performed (the bravado that hides the same old fear). The gap between how old someone *acts* and how old they *are* is pure character.
- **What age does to confidence:** the young often over-perform to hide uncertainty; the older often under-perform to avoid seeming desperate. Know which one you're writing.

## Age Gaps & Power (the charge, handled well)

An age gap is one of the most reliable sources of heat in smut — because it *is* a power dynamic before anyone touches:

- **The older partner** brings experience, authority, the eroticism of being *known and handled* — or of being the one who finally lets themselves be wanted.
- **The younger partner** brings hunger, nerve, the eroticism of being *chosen and taught* — or of being the one who teaches the older person something new.
- **The inversion** — the older one who's innocent, the younger one who's the teacher — is often the most interesting version, because it breaks the expected script.
- **Write the person, not the gap.** The gap matters because of what it *does* — the imbalance, the thrill of the forbidden, the tenderness of being met at different speeds — not as a number to announce. "He was twice her age" is a stat; "she'd never been looked at like she was the only person who'd ever taught anyone anything" is a scene.

**The line:** the gap is hot *between adults*. If the power imbalance is written to make one character feel like a child, you've left the adult frame — pull back to the adults they are.

## Age & Voice

A character's age lives in their voice — what they reference, how they speak, what they assume:

- **Reference points** — the music, the slang, the technology, the cultural touchstones of *their* formative years. A character who grew up before smartphones notices different things than one who didn't.
- **Register & confidence** — the young often hedge ("I mean, I guess?"), the older often state. But beware cliché: not every older character is formal, not every young one is slangy.
- **What they call things** — the vocabulary of their generation for bodies, for sex, for love. Voice is a timestamp, used lightly.

## How Age Feeds the Desire Engine

Age is not decoration — it *is* the engine, in disguise:

- **The newly-adult** character's want is often *permission* — to want openly, to be taken seriously as a desiring person.
- **The mid-life** character's want is often *reclamation* — the thing they denied themselves for twenty years.
- **The older** character's want is often *proof* — that they're still desired, still vital, still worth wanting back.

Match the age to the want and the scene writes itself. A 19-year-old who needs permission and a 45-year-old who needs reclamation are two different engines — and put together, a duet.

## Vocabulary

- **Stages:** *young, newly adult, in their twenties/thirties/forties, mid-life, older, mature, seasoned, silver, later in life*
- **Experience:** *first time, inexperienced, learning, new to this, experienced, skilled, knows what they want, been around, self-possessed*
- **The gap:** *older, younger, the age difference, the years between them, twice her age, a decade older, old enough to be —*
- **The feeling:** *permission, reclamation, proof, being seen, being chosen, being taught, being known, being still wanted*

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Age as a number** — "he was 34." The number is the least interesting part; write the stage, the experience, the hunger.
2. **Aging everyone the same** — a cast where everyone is implicitly 25 and interchangeable. Vary the stages; each brings a different engine.
3. **Infantilizing the younger partner** — writing an adult as a childlike innocent to heighten the gap. They're an adult; give them adult agency (see the main skill's "Agency, always").
4. **The clichéd older partner** — "silver fox" or "cougar" as a costume with nothing inside. Age them into a *person*, not a trope.
5. **Forgetting the adult frame** — any character under 18, or age-play that drifts into depicting a minor. Hard line: adults only, always.
6. **Voice that doesn't match the age** — a 20-year-old who speaks like a 60-year-old (or vice versa) without that being the point.
