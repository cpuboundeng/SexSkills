# PREFERENCES

## Overview

Preferences are what a character *likes* — in sex, in partners, in the shape of desire itself. They're the difference between a character who "likes sex" (meaningless) and a character who "goes weak at a hand on the back of her neck, needs it slow before it gets rough, and will not, under any circumstances, do it with the lights on" (a person). Preferences are how a character's desire gets *specific* — and specificity is what makes a scene feel real.

**Key distinction:** Preferences aren't a checklist. They're a *taste* — a point of view on pleasure that's as individual as a fingerprint. Write them as character, not as inventory.

## Sexual Preferences (the specifics)

- **Acts they love** — and *how* they love them (fast, slow, rough, gentle, loud, quiet).
- **Pace & intensity** — some need the slow build; some want to be pinned and taken. The pace a character prefers *is* their personality in the bedroom.
- **Dynamics** — who leads, who follows, who likes to be surprised. (Cross-ref the `kinks.md` for the deeper kink profile; this is the everyday taste.)
- **Turn-ons** — be *specific and a little weird*. "He likes being bitten on the shoulder." "She likes when someone holds her face while kissing her." "He likes the sound of his name." Weird, specific turn-ons make a character memorable.
- **Turn-offs / dealbreakers** — the things that kill it for them. A character's "no" list is as revealing as their "yes" list.

## Experience & Confidence

- **Inexperienced** — first times, nerves, discovery, the tenderness (or awkwardness) of learning a body.
- **Experienced** — ease, skill, a body that knows what it wants and asks for it.
- **Confident vs. shy** — confidence can be real or performed; shyness can coexist with a fierce private hunger. The gap between how they *present* and what they *want* is pure engine.

## Libido & What Gets Them Going

- **High / low / responsive** — a character's baseline desire, and whether they spark spontaneously or need a trigger.
- **What turns the key** — emotional connection, physical touch, a power shift, danger, novelty, being wanted. Know what *specific* thing flips them from ordinary to hungry.

## What They Want in a Partner

- **Type (loosely)** — tall, soft-spoken, funny, dangerous, kind. Types are allowed; they're real. Just don't let the type *replace* the person (see `race_ethnicity.md`'s desire frame).
- **Qualities** — someone who can make them laugh, someone who'll call them out, someone safe, someone dangerous.
- **The intangible** — "someone who looks at me like I'm the only person in the room." The thing they can't articulate but recognize instantly.

## Romantic vs. Sexual

- **Orientation** — see `gender_sexuality.md`. Note that romantic and sexual orientation can differ.
- **Relationship style** — monogamous, polyamorous, open, casual, "I don't do relationships" (often a lie the story will test).
- **Romantic style** — sweeping vs. pragmatic, all-in vs. guarded, romantic vs. allergic to romance. How does this person *love*?

## Love Language (how they give and receive)

The five, as character shorthand — and note that giving and receiving languages often differ:

- **Words** — needs to *hear* it; says it freely or can't say it at all.
- **Touch** — the hand on the knee, the absent caress; touch is their proof.
- **Acts of service** — shows love by doing; the coffee made just right, the car fixed.
- **Gifts** — the small thoughtful thing; not about money, about being thought of.
- **Time** — presence is the gift; being there, fully.

A character whose love language is *words* and whose partner's is *service* has a built-in miscommunication — and a built-in arc.

## How Preferences Drive Scenes & Chemistry

- **A preference, dramatized.** "She likes being bitten" is a note. "She likes being bitten, and he can't bring himself to hurt her, so she has to *ask* for it" is a scene. Preferences become story when they collide with another character.
- **Complement vs. conflict** — complementary preferences = smooth heat; conflicting ones = tension. Both are useful; know which you're writing.
- **Show the preference in action.** Don't state it in narration and never use it. If a character "likes being dominated," *show* them melting the moment someone takes the lead.

## Vocabulary

- **Tastes:** *likes, craves, goes weak for, can't resist, needs, prefers, won't, can't, would rather, has a thing for*
- **Turn-ons:** *the hand at the back of the neck, being bitten, the sound of their name, being held down, being chased, being watched*
- **Turn-offs:** *sloppiness, a wandering eye, being rushed, being called pet names, the lights on*

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Generic tastes** — "likes foreplay, dislikes pain." Meaningless. Get specific and weird.
2. **Preferences that never appear** — stated in the profile, absent from the scene. Show them.
3. **"I like everything"** — that's not a taste, that's the absence of a character.
4. **Preferences as plot armor** — a character whose every desire is conveniently met. Let preferences *conflict* sometimes.
5. **No turn-offs** — a character with no limits has no edge. The "no" is as revealing as the "yes."
