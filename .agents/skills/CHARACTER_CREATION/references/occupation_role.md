# OCCUPATION & ROLE

## Overview

What a character *does* is who they are in the world — and in smut, it's also the engine of half the scenarios you'll ever write. The job gives a character status, money, time, competence, and — most usefully — a *power position* that the bedroom can reinforce or invert. A CEO and a waitress aren't just two jobs; they're two different relationships to power, to fatigue, to being looked at.

**Key distinction:** Occupation is **context and scenario-fuel, not costume.** A character isn't "a doctor" — they're a specific person *who is a doctor*: exhausted by it, proud of it, trapped in it, or quietly done with it. The job is a door into who they are, not a label that replaces them.

## Why the Job Matters

- **Status & power** — what the job says about where they sit in the world, and what it lets them command (or not).
- **Money & class** — the invisible layer under everything: what they can afford, how they dress, where they live, how they're treated.
- **Time & energy** — the night shift, the 80-hour week, the slow season. What work *costs* them shows up in the body and the mood.
- **Competence** — the eroticism of watching someone be *good at something*. Skill is hot: the surgeon's steady hands, the chef's knife-work, the mechanic who knows exactly what's wrong.
- **Where they're seen** — some jobs make a person visible (the performer, the public figure); some make them invisible (the cleaner, the night crew). Visibility shapes desire.

## The Job as Character

A person's work is a series of choices and constraints, and it reveals them:

- **Chosen vs. fallen into** — the one who always wanted this vs. the one who ended up here. Do they love it, hate it, or carry it like a duty?
- **The job as identity vs. the job as paycheck** — some people *are* their work; some clock out and become someone else entirely. Which one are they?
- **The mismatch** — the character whose job contradicts their desire: the buttoned-up accountant who's a dom at night, the preschool teacher who craves degradation. Mismatch is where the heat lives.
- **The secret of the work** — the thing the job taught them about people, about bodies, about power, that nobody else knows.

## Occupation as Scenario Engine

Most erotic roleplay scenarios *are* occupations — the job creates the setting, the power gap, and the excuse for two people to be in a room together:

- **The power pairings** — boss/employee, doctor/patient, professor/student, officer/suspect, landlord/tenant, star/fan, coach/athlete. The job *is* the dynamic.
- **The service & care** — the massage therapist, the nurse, the personal trainer, the housekeeper, the bartender. Work that involves hands, care, and bodies is a natural bridge to intimacy.
- **The danger & authority** — the firefighter, the soldier, the bodyguard, the criminal, the detective. Risk and command.
- **The "off-duty" moment** — the uniform coming off, the scrubs, the apron, the tie loosened after hours. The transition from "at work" to "off the clock" is a scene in itself. (See `roleplay` for the full scenario catalog and `style_dress.md` for the workwear.)

## The Power Gap (and its inversion)

The single most useful thing an occupation gives you is a **power difference** — and the best smut plays with it:

- **Reinforced** — the power dynamic of the job carries into the bedroom (the boss stays the boss).
- **Inverted** — the bedroom flips the job's hierarchy: the CEO who kneels, the handyman who takes charge, the person everyone defers to who just wants to be *handled*. The inversion is often hotter than the reinforcement, because it reveals what the character never gets to show at work.
- **The equalizer** — the job's power is stripped away entirely, and two people meet as just two people. Also a move.

Ask: *does the bedroom confirm or flip what the job says about this person?* Either answer is a scene; the interesting one is usually the flip.

## Class & Money (the invisible layer)

Class is the part of occupation that people feel but rarely say:

- **How class shows** — voice, word choice, comfort with money, what they order, how they tip, whether they apologize for taking up space. (See `personality_backstory.md` on voice.)
- **Class distance between partners** — the rich one and the one who's never had it easy. The gap is charge: condescension, gratitude, resentment, the desire to be wanted *despite* or *because of* it.
- **Write the person, not the category.** "Rich" and "poor" are not personalities. A rich character can be generous or cruel or oblivious; a poor one can be proud or bitter or free. Give them the specifics.

## What Work Does to the Body

Occupations leave marks — and marks are characterization (and material for tenderness):

- **The hands** — calluses, ink, grease under the nails, the soft hands of someone who never lifts anything. Hands are the first thing a lover notices; work writes on them.
- **The posture & fatigue** — the desk-worker's stiff neck, the nurse's aching feet, the dancer's long muscles, the laborer's tired shoulders.
- **The smell** — sawdust, coffee, bleach, motor oil, hospital soap, perfume from the counter. Work has a scent, and scent is memory (see `appearance.md`).

These are the details that make a character's body *this* body, shaped by *this* life.

## Vocabulary

- **The roles:** *boss, employee, doctor, nurse, professor, student, officer, suspect, star, fan, coach, athlete, trainer, therapist, bartender, chef, mechanic, artist, performer, executive, laborer, service worker*
- **The status:** *in charge, overworked, underpaid, ambitious, settled, trapped, proud, exhausted, off-duty, on the clock*
- **The power:** *authority, rank, the chain of command, the one who signs the checks, the one everyone answers to, the one nobody sees*
- **The marks:** *callused hands, tired feet, a stiff neck, ink-stained fingers, the smell of —, the uniform, the scrubs, the apron, the tie*

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Job as costume** — "she's a lawyer" with nothing about what the law does to her. The job must shape the person.
2. **Occupation without power** — naming a job but ignoring the status, money, and hierarchy it carries. The power is the point.
3. **The same-old boss/secretary** — a trope with no specificity. Give the boss a reason to be the boss, the secretary a life beyond the desk.
4. **No off-duty self** — a character who's only ever "at work." Show who they are when the uniform comes off.
5. **Class as caricature** — rich = cold, poor = noble, with nothing between. Write the individual.
6. **Forgetting the body** — a job that leaves no mark on the hands, the posture, the smell. Work writes on the body; use it.
