# CHARACTER CREATION

## Overview

A smut scene only works if the reader cares about the *people* having it. Two interchangeable bodies — "a tall man," "a curvy woman" — can go through any act in this library and still land flat, because there's nothing to root for. A character, by contrast, is someone you can *imagine wanting something*. That wanting is what makes the sex matter.

So the job of character creation is not to assemble a list of physical traits. It's to build a **desire engine**: a person with wants, needs, a secret, an obstacle, and a voice — someone whose presence on the page makes the reader lean in. The body, the kinks, the preferences are all in service of that engine; they're the *surface* through which the wanting shows.

**Key distinction:** A character is not a stat block. "5'10, blue eyes, likes BDSM" is a profile, not a person. The same trait list produces a dozen different people depending on *what they want and what they're afraid of*. Start with the engine; the details follow.

## The Desire Engine (the core)

Every compelling smut character answers these five questions. Answer them *specifically* — vagueness is the enemy.

1. **What do they want?** — the conscious desire: a person, a feeling, a moment of control, to be wanted back.
2. **What do they need?** — the deeper want, sometimes the opposite of what they chase.
3. **What won't they admit?** — the shame, the secret, the thing they'd never say aloud. The heat lives here.
4. **What's in the way?** — the obstacle: fear, pride, a partner, their own inhibition, a past wound.
5. **What would it take to break?** — the moment the obstacle gives. This is the scene you'll write.

**Worked example:** A character who *wants* to be dominated (conscious) but *needs* to feel safe enough to surrender (deeper), *won't admit* she's exhausted from always being the one in charge (secret), is *blocked* by pride and a bad past relationship (obstacle), and will *break* the first time someone notices she's tired and simply takes over without asking (the scene). Now she's a person. Her body and kinks just dress the engine.

## The Principles

- **Specificity beats beauty.** A character who "looks like a model" is forgettable. A character with a crooked incisor, a scar from a childhood fall, a habit of rolling their sleeves to exactly three-quarters — that's a person. One precise, human detail does more than a paragraph of superlatives.
- **Contradiction makes them real.** Give them one thing that shouldn't fit: the dom who's gentle in the aftercare, the confident flirt who freezes at real intimacy, the big scary one who blushes. Contradiction is where desire gets interesting.
- **Desire has a face.** Write every trait through how it *reads* to someone who wants them — the stretch of a shirt across shoulders, the way a laugh changes a face. Appearance is filtered through hunger, not narrated like a driver's license.
- **The secret is the engine.** The thing they won't admit — about what they want, or who they want — is the single most useful thing you'll invent. It makes their scenes *theirs* and no one else's.
- **Agency, always.** A character isn't a body that things happen to. They want, choose, hesitate, push, pull, and sometimes misread the moment. Give them hands and a voice — even, especially, when they're submitting.

## Chemistry (two characters are better than one)

Most smut is a duet. Two rich characters who don't spark off each other are two lonely monologues. Build the *reaction*:

- **What does each one notice about the other — that no one else would?** The detail only *this* person would clock, because of who *this* person is.
- **What do they want from each other?** And do those wants line up, or pull in opposite directions?
- **Where's the friction?** Complementary kinks = smooth heat; conflicting wants = tension. Both work — but *know which you're writing*.
- **What's the obstacle between them?** A power gap, a history, a misunderstanding, a rule they're both about to break.

The most reliable trick: make the two characters' desires *fit together imperfectly* — enough overlap to draw them together, enough mismatch to keep the scene from being frictionless and dull.

## The Character Sheet

A fill-in template. Not a cage — a starting point.

```
Name / nickname (and what it means to them):
Identity: gender, pronouns, sexuality —
Heritage: race/ethnicity, culture, the details that matter —
Body: shape, size, one thing they love/hate about it —
Appearance: hair, eyes, skin, the first thing people notice —
Voice: how they talk, the words they use, what they'd never say —
Personality: two strengths, two flaws, one contradiction —
Desire: what they want —
Need: what they actually need —
Secret: what they won't admit —
Wound: the past thing that shaped their desire —
Obstacle: what's in the way —
Preferences: turn-ons, turn-offs, experience, what they want in a partner —
Kinks: what they're into, why, limits, and what it reveals —
The break: the moment the obstacle gives —
```

## Writing Tips

- **Show the body through the wanting.** Don't stop the scene to describe; let the POV character *notice* — the hollow of a throat, the heat through a shirt. (See the `skill-style-guide` for show-don't-tell.)
- **One specific detail beats ten generic ones.** Pick the *one* thing about this person that no one else has, and return to it.
- **Let voice do characterization.** A character's speech and inner monologue should sound like *them* — word choice, rhythm, what they notice. (See the `skill-style-guide` and `verbal-erotica`.)
- **Write the body as loved, not judged.** Even flaws — scars, softness, asymmetry — are described as *desirable*, because the POV character desires them. The reader follows the wanting.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **The stat block.** A list of measurements with no desire behind it. Start with the engine.
2. **No want, no obstacle.** A character with nothing at stake and nothing in the way is set dressing.
3. **The "perfect" character.** Flawless bodies and flawless personalities are boring — and read as author wish-fulfillment, not a person.
4. **Cardboard by default.** "Generic attractive" with no specific detail. Give them one memorable thing.
5. **Stereotype instead of person.** Reducing a character to a category (race, gender, body type, kink) — see `references/race_ethnicity.md` and `references/gender_sexuality.md`.
6. **Over-description.** A full-page head-to-toe before anything happens kills momentum. Drip the details through the scene.
7. **No agency.** A character who just receives. Let them want, choose, and act.
8. **Same voice for everyone.** If two characters sound interchangeable, neither is a person.
