# ROLEPLAY

## Overview

Erotic roleplay is the performance of a *fantasy scenario* — two (or more) partners stepping into invented roles and playing out a scripted dynamic for the duration of the scene. The costume, the persona, the borrowed authority: all of it exists to unlock desires that don't fit into everyday life. In fiction, roleplay scenes are the ultimate *layered* writing challenge — you're writing characters who are themselves playing characters.

The erotic power of roleplay is **permission through pretend**. The "doctor" can say things the real person can't; the "stranger" can be bolder; the "boss" can demand. The invented frame gives everyone an alibi to be someone else — someone with different boundaries, different power, different desires. And because it's *pretend*, it carries a built-in safety valve: the fantasy can go anywhere precisely because it isn't "real."

**Key distinction:** Roleplay is *not* the same as a spontaneous power dynamic. It's a *staged* scene with agreed personas and a shared script (loose or tight). The characters agree — even implicitly — to *be* these roles for a while. That agreement is what makes the taboo safe and the scene hot.

## The Scenario Catalog

### Authority & Power
- **Boss / employee** — the performance review that goes off-script; the late night at the office; the "one-on-one meeting." Power is institutional, legible, and deliciously inappropriate.
- **Teacher / student** — the after-class "extra credit," the detention that becomes something else. The taboo of authority *and* age.
- **Doctor / patient** — the "examination" frame: clinical language turning warm, professional detachment crumbling. The built-in vulnerability of the patient role.
- **Police / detainee** — the "pat-down," the interrogation, the plea bargain. Uniforms, authority, and the threat of consequence.
- **Officer / soldier / captain** — rank, discipline, and orders. The chain of command as erotic ladder.

### Service & Status
- **Boss / maid or servant** — the "you missed a spot" inspection, the below-stairs power gap, service and submission.
- **Butler / aristocrat** — decorum, formality, and the slow crack in the polished facade.
- **Personal trainer / client** — the "let me spot you" proximity, the shared sweat, the coach's authority over the body.

### Strangers & Taboo
- **Stranger at a bar** — the pick-up played as performance, the thrill of being *unknown* to each other.
- **Delivery person / homeowner** — the "come in for a minute" pretext, the transactional opening.
- **Stranger in danger** — the "stranded traveler," the "storm brought me in" setup; rescue and gratitude as fuel.

### Fantasy & Genre
- **Vampire / victim** — predation, immortality, the bite as penetration. Gothic desire.
- **Knight / royalty** — chivalry, courtship, and the forbidden beneath the armor.
- **Alien / abductee** — the clinical "examination" made literal and alien; otherness and curiosity.
- **Omegaverse roles** — see the `cum-play` skill; alpha/omega dynamics are a built-in roleplay frame.

## The Anatomy of a Roleplay Scene

### 1. The Agreement (explicit or implicit)
How do the characters *enter* the roles? The best scenes show this beat — even briefly:
- A negotiated setup ("You be the doctor, I'll be the patient").
- A spontaneous slide (a joke becomes a dynamic; "yes, *sir*" slips out).
- A signal or prop (a costume put on, a title used, a door closed).

The agreement is the *permission* beat. Showing it — a nod, a smile, a "remember the safeword" — grounds the scene and makes the taboo safe.

### 2. The Persona
Who are they *as* the role? The persona is a *heightened* version of the self, or a full invention:
- **The character voice** — how does the role speak? Clipped and clinical, warm and teasing, cold and commanding.
- **The costume/props** — a lab coat, a tie, a uniform, an accent. Details that *sell* the illusion.
- **The motivation** — what does the persona want? The boss wants obedience; the stranger wants the chase; the doctor wants to "examine."

### 3. The Power Structure
Roleplay scenarios are almost always *power* scenarios. Identify the axis:
- **Who holds authority?** (boss, doctor, teacher, officer)
- **Who is vulnerable?** (employee, patient, student, detainee)
- **Does the power flip mid-scene?** The patient who turns the tables, the employee who takes charge. Power reversal is the classic mid-scene twist.

### 4. The In-Character Arc
The scene *plays out* the fantasy. Key beats:
- **The pretext** — the excuse that brings them together ("I need to examine you").
- **The escalation** — the pretext thins, the real desire shows through.
- **The breaking of decorum** — the role cracks: the doctor's hands linger, the boss loosens their tie, the teacher's voice drops.
- **The climax** — the fantasy fulfilled, in or out of character.

### 5. The Exit (the landing)
How does the scene *end*? This matters as much as the beginning:
- **Staying in character** — the roles persist to the last line ("You're free to go, detective").
- **Breaking character** — the masks drop, the real people surface, often in laughter or tenderness. The contrast is powerful.
- **Aftercare** — especially after power-heavy or degrading scenarios. The return to "real" selves is part of the scene's emotional arc.

## The Layered Writing Problem

Roleplay fiction is *doubled*. You're writing:
1. **The outer layer** — the real characters, their relationship, their real desires.
2. **The inner layer** — the personas, the invented dynamic, the scripted fantasy.
3. **The bleed-through** — where the two layers touch: a real emotion leaking into the persona, a persona's confidence lingering after the mask drops.

**The most powerful moments are the seams:**
- The real character, startled by how much they *liked* playing the role.
- The persona saying something that's actually *true* for the real person underneath.
- The moment the fantasy touches a real nerve — and the characters decide whether to keep going or stop.

Write *both* layers, and especially the friction between them. That's what elevates roleplay smut from "two people in costume" to a story about desire, identity, and trust.

## Dirty Talk in Roleplay

See the `verbal-erotica` skill for the full treatment. In roleplay, the key is **staying in the persona's voice**:
- The doctor is clinical-then-warm: "You're doing very well. Just breathe. Good patient."
- The boss is clipped and commanding: "You're behind on your deadlines. We'll address that now."
- The stranger is bold and direct: "I saw you watching me across the bar."

The *persona's* vocabulary is what sells the fantasy. A single line in the right voice does more than a paragraph of stage direction.

## Writing Tips

### Sensory checklist
- **Sight:** the costume, the props, the setting dressed for the scene (the office after hours, the exam room, the empty classroom)
- **Sound:** the persona's voice, the accent or register, the door closing, the title spoken aloud
- **Touch:** the prop (a tie loosened, a lab coat's crisp fabric), the first physical boundary crossed
- **Interiority:** what the *real* character is thinking beneath the persona — the excitement, the nerves, the surprise at their own hunger

### Vocabulary
**The setup:** *cast, play, pretend, become, step into, dress up, take the part, in character, the game, the scenario*
**The persona's frame:** *role, persona, mask, disguise, performance, act, scene, script*
**The bleed-through:** *slip, crack, break character, surface, real, beneath, underneath*

### Common pitfalls
1. **Skipping the agreement** — roleplay without a consent beat reads as actual abuse. Show the permission, even briefly.
2. **One layer only** — writing only the persona (flat, empty) or only the real person (no fantasy). The *double* layer is the art.
3. **Costume over character** — a "doctor" with no clinical voice is just someone in a coat. Ground the persona in speech and behavior.
4. **Forgetting the exit** — how the scene ends (in character or out) is a crucial emotional beat. Don't abandon the masks mid-air without resolving it.
5. **Unclear power axis** — every roleplay has a power structure. Know who holds it, and whether it flips.

## Scene Structure Example

1. **The Agreement** — A raised eyebrow, a grin. "You want to play?" / "Be the boss. I'll be the new hire." The safeword is settled in a glance. The game begins.

2. **The Pretext** — The "boss" adjusts their tie, all business. "Close the door. We need to discuss your performance." The "employee" swallows, plays along. "Yes, sir."

3. **The Escalation** — The performance review turns pointed. The boss steps closer, voice dropping. The pretext thins: "I think there's something else you're here for."

4. **The Break** — A hand on the desk, a sharp intake of breath. The boss's professional mask cracks — a flicker of the *real* person underneath, hungry and surprised by it. "God, I've wanted this."

5. **The Fulfillment** — The fantasy plays out in full — the boss demanding, the employee yielding, both of them fully in the invented world.

6. **The Exit** — Afterward, the tie is loose, the roles dissolving. A laugh, shaky and real. "Okay. Okay, that was —" The masks drop, and the real two people surface, grinning, breathless, closer than before.

7. **The Landing** — The game is over, but something lingers: a new confidence, a new understanding. The fantasy changed them, just a little. That's the point.
