# SEX

## Overview

Every sex scene is defined by two axes:

- **The TYPE of sex** — its emotional register. Why is this happening, what does it mean to the people involved, and what does it leave behind? The slow, tender connection of a Sunday morning and the urgent, breathless reunion after weeks apart are not the same experience, even if the mechanics look identical.
- **The POSITION** — its physical shape. Who is where, who controls depth and rhythm, what angles and sensations are on the table, and what the arrangement communicates.

Type × Position = Scene. A romantic reunion in spooning is a completely different scene than the same spooning done in anger. Choosing both deliberately — and knowing what each choice communicates — is the difference between a sex scene that advances the story and one that just changes the furniture.

This skill gives you the full emotional taxonomy (24 types, drawn from the psychology of intimacy) and the physical vocabulary (the positions, from foundational to expanded), each with the sensations, dynamics, and writing angles that make them land.

## Scene Structure

The sex scene arc — use it as a skeleton, bend it to the scene's type:

1. **The ignition** — the look, the word, the touch that flips the scene on. The *type* of sex is often set here: a question, a glance, an unspoken need.
2. **The approach** — clothing coming off, the first bare contact, the space between bodies closing. Slow this down; this is where tension banks.
3. **The build** — rhythm, sounds, the body waking up. The position chosen (or shifting) as desire dictates.
4. **The peak** — climax, or the moment the scene's type declares itself (the confession mid-thrust, the tears, the laugh).
5. **The release** — the body letting go; the immediate physical aftermath.
6. **The landing** — what the scene *meant*. The aftermath carries the emotional payload of the type: romantic lands in held closeness, casual lands in the quiet question, angry lands in the tension still unresolved.

> See the `aftercare` skill for the landing itself, and the type references for how each type reshapes this arc.

## Writing Sex Scenes

### Match the Type to the Story
Ask: what does this scene *do* for the characters? A first-time scene and a make-up-for-lost-time scene could describe identical movements but must be written completely differently — the first trembles with uncertainty, the second floods with relief. Let the type drive word choice, pacing, and the landing.

### Sensory Checklist
- **Sight**: the map of a body — the line of a spine, the arch of a back, the flex of thighs; flushed skin; the slide of a silhouette against a window; where their bodies meet and where they don't
- **Sound**: breath, the shift of sheets, wet sounds, a name said differently, the room's quiet as much as its noise
- **Touch**: heat, the give of skin, the grip of fingers, the drag of fabric, weight and resistance
- **Smell**: skin, hair, sweat, the particular scent of a person after a day
- **Taste**: salt, the inside of a kiss, the flavor of a neck, of skin after exertion

### Tone Calibration
- **Romantic/tender**: long sentences, soft verbs, eye contact, the world shrinking to two bodies
- **Urgent/desperate**: short sentences, fragmented thought, the body moving before the mind
- **Playful**: dialogue, laughter, imperfect choreography, the shared joke
- **Rough/primal**: animal verbs, sounds over words, instinct
- **Sorrowful/liminal** (breakup, pity, closure): the gap between what the bodies do and what the hearts feel
- **Comfort/quiet**: gentle, slow, the scene as a warm blanket

### Vocabulary
- **Beginning**: *catch, glance, reach, pull, tug, shed, bare, close, hover, part*
- **Building**: *press, slide, rock, grind, thrust, arch, grip, grip tighter, shudder, tremble, gasp, moan*
- **Climax**: *peak, crest, break, flood, seize, pulse, come undone, shatter, spill*
- **Landing**: *settle, sink, soften, hold, stroke, murmur, drift, stay*

### Common Mistakes
| Mistake | Why It Weakens the Scene | Fix |
|---------|--------------------------|-----|
| Same register for every scene | The type isn't chosen; every scene reads the same | Pick the type deliberately; let it drive pacing and landing |
| Position as decoration | "They had sex in missionary" — so what? | Use the position's dynamics: who controls, what's seen, what's felt |
| No emotional consequence | The scene ends at orgasm; nothing changed | Write the landing — what the scene meant |
| Performed perfection | No awkwardness, no laughter, no adjustment | Real bodies negotiate, shift, sometimes laugh |
| Ignoring the character's type | A pity scene written like a romantic one rings false | The type determines the interiority |
| Formulaic build | Every scene is a straight line to climax | Build in waves; the type sets the wave shape |
| No negotiation of dynamics | Power and consent invisible | Even one line of established trust changes everything |
